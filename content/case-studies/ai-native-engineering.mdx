<Section name="context">

Across pilot projects, measured from the delivery workflow's own state transitions, cycle time fell 30 to 40% and delivery throughput rose 25 to 30%. Across the same pilot projects, active adoption sat above 95%, from a survey the team answered itself.

Those results are why this page exists, so they come first. The capability behind them was AI-native engineering: making AI a governed, repeatable part of how the team specified, built, reviewed and tested software, rather than scattered individual experiments, and being able to prove whether it worked.

The team was established, and much of it came from a legacy on-premise background. The setting was a regulated clinical environment, where ungoverned AI use carries real compliance risk.

</Section>

<Section name="actions">

- **The governance pack.** An AI policy, risk profiles, a register of use cases, a register of approved tools, operational guidelines and training. It went through the AI governance board review, with every comment answered against a published traceability matrix, and it is what unblocked the capability.
- **The engineering side.** Custom agent skills and reusable commands, engineering conventions, project memory, source-controlled standards, integrations and agentic workflows, shared across the team rather than reinvented by each engineer.
- **Specification-driven development.** Requirements became version-controlled specifications that generate epics, features, user stories and implementation scaffolds. The same specifications give AI-assisted design, implementation, review and testing consistent context, and plan-then-apply guardrails keep every AI change under mandatory human review.
- **The measurement framework.** Metrics for productivity, quality, adoption and throughput, defined on the delivery workflow's own state transitions. It is informed by DORA but adapted for AI-assisted delivery, with an adoption category DORA has no equivalent for.
- **A governed retrieval layer, which I built and ran.** Vector search tells the assistant what is relevant. Authority tells it what wins when relevant sources conflict, and expiry tells it when something must stop winning. When a service was found not to be safe to retry, a temporary exception to the resilience standard went into the index with an expiry, until the team that owned the service fixed it. Once the fix landed, the exception expired and left the index, and the change that restored the retry passed the AI code review without objection. A plain similarity search would have kept ranking the dead exception near the top, because it named the service, the pattern and the reasoning, and the reviewer would have objected to the very change that put things right.

</Section>

<Section name="lessons">

The evidence has a limit, and I name it before anyone asks. The sample was favourable, and adoption is an input measure, not business value: the adoption figure covered everything from engineers running the whole lifecycle through agents to engineers using one skill for code review. My view is that the results are achievable more broadly, but I have not proven that.

</Section>
